Where the colors sit on the color wheel

The wheel shows the hue of each palette color. The letters mark Primary, Secondary, and Accent, and the white line connects them so you can see the shape of the harmony: a straight line for complementary, a short arc for analogous, and a triangle for triadic.

    Hue is measured in degrees around the wheel: 0° is red, 120° is green, and 240° is blue.

    Color theory basics

    The vocabulary behind every choice in this project. The dots in this section are teaching examples, so they stay the same when the palette changes.

    Primary colors

    The starting colors that cannot be made by mixing others. Painters use red, yellow, and blue; screens mix red, green, and blue light.

    Secondary colors

    Made by mixing two primaries: orange (red + yellow), green (yellow + blue), and purple (blue + red).

    Tertiary colors

    A primary mixed with the secondary beside it, giving names like red-orange, yellow-green, and blue-violet.

    Warm colors

    Reds, oranges, and yellows. They feel energetic and close, and they draw the eye first.

    Cool colors

    Blues, greens, and violets. They feel calm and spacious, and they tend to sit back on the page.

    Hue

    The pure color family, such as red or blue, and its position around the wheel in degrees.

    Saturation

    How intense a color is. Low saturation looks gray and muted; high saturation looks vivid.

    Brightness / value

    How light or dark a color is. Value differences are what make text readable on a background.

    Color wheel

    Hues arranged in a circle so their relationships are easy to see. Harmonies are patterns drawn on it, like the shapes in the wheel section above.

    Color analysis

    Palette 1: Complementary — Teal & Coral Red

    Harmony

    Complementary. The accent sits exactly opposite the primary on the wheel (175° and 355°, a 180° gap). The secondary is a lighter shade of the primary hue, so only the accent uses the opposite color.

    RoleColorHEXHue
    Primary#0A5751175° (deep teal)
    Secondary#2B827B175° (sea teal)
    Accent#CE2230355° (signal red)
    Background#F4FBFAteal-tinted white
    Text#0D2120teal-tinted black
    Surface#E3F2F1light teal tint

    Why I selected these colors

    A deep teal makes a steady, trustworthy base, and red is the one color that cuts through it. Because the two are opposites, the red accent looks brighter next to teal than it would on its own. I kept the accent small so the contrast feels like a spark instead of a fight, and I tinted the background and text toward teal so the neutrals belong to the palette.

    Mood

    Energetic but professional. Confident, modern, and clear about what to click.

    Website usage

    I would use the primary teal for the header, hero, and main headings, the secondary teal for navigation and supporting buttons, and the red accent only for the most important call-to-action button and small highlights. The near-white background holds the main content, with the near-black teal text on top for easy reading.

    Palette 2: Analogous — Blue, Indigo & Violet

    Harmony

    Analogous. Three neighboring hues, each 30° from the next: blue (210°), indigo (240°), and violet (270°). Because they sit side by side, they blend into one smooth family.

    RoleColorHEXHue
    Primary#0F4C8A210° (ocean blue)
    Secondary#3030A6240° (indigo)
    Accent#7F26D9270° (vivid violet)
    Background#F7F8FDblue-tinted white
    Text#0E152Amidnight navy
    Surface#E8ECF7light blue tint

    Why I selected these colors

    I wanted to see how far one design could go with almost no color conflict. Moving from blue to violet keeps every color related, so nothing clashes. Since neighbors have less hue contrast, I used bigger jumps in brightness and saturation to keep the parts distinct: the accent is the brightest, most saturated of the three.

    Mood

    Calm, focused, and cohesive, with a creative, slightly dreamy feel from the violet end.

    Website usage

    I would use the ocean blue for the header and main headings, the indigo for navigation and secondary elements, and the violet accent for buttons and links. This suits a tech, education, or wellness site where a smooth, quiet look matters more than strong contrast.

    Palette 3: Triadic — Berry Rose, Azure & Lime

    Harmony

    Triadic. Three hues evenly spaced 120° apart: rose (330°), azure blue (210°), and lime green (90°). Together they form a triangle on the wheel, so the palette is colorful but balanced.

    RoleColorHEXHue
    Primary#9E1A5C330° (berry rose)
    Secondary#125CA5210° (azure)
    Accent#80C93690° (lime)
    Background#FAF5F7blush-tinted white
    Text#250E1Adeep plum
    Surface#F3E8EDlight rose tint

    Why I selected these colors

    With a triad, one color should lead and the other two support it, or the page turns chaotic. Berry rose leads, azure supports it, and lime is used in small doses. Lime is a very light color, so I only use it as a fill with dark text on top (it is too pale to read as text on the light background). The tinted neutrals keep the bold hues from feeling harsh.

    Mood

    Playful, bold, and creative. It feels youthful and full of variety.

    Website usage

    I would use the berry rose for the header and hero, azure for navigation and secondary buttons, and the lime accent for call-to-action buttons and badges. This would fit a creative portfolio, an event page, or a brand that wants to stand out.

    What I learned

    What makes two colors work together?

    A clear relationship on the wheel, plus enough difference in brightness that each color can be seen.

    What happens with opposite colors?

    Strong contrast and energy. They make each other look brighter, so they work best when one is used sparingly.

    What happens with neighboring colors?

    A smooth, cohesive look. It is calm and safe, but needs value differences to keep parts distinct.

    How does color change a site's mood?

    The layout never changed between palettes, yet the feel went from professional to calm to playful using color alone.
